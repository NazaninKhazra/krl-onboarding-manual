# 8. AI-Use Policy

Generative AI tools can help with coding, editing, and searching the literature. They also produce errors that look correct, and they can expose data that must stay private. This policy sets out how members may use these tools in lab work. It applies to chatbots, coding assistants, and AI features built into other software.

## University rules

The University of Toronto's School of Graduate Studies states that unauthorized use of generative AI in scholarly work may be an offence under the Code of Behaviour on Academic Matters, and may be research misconduct under the Policy on Ethical Conduct in Research. This policy sets out what is authorized in K.R.L. Anything it does not allow is unauthorized.

The University's Centre for Research & Innovation Support also notes that content created in University-provided AI tools may be accessible to third parties through legal processes, including Ontario's Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act (FIPPA). Treat anything you enter into an AI tool, including University-licensed tools such as Microsoft Copilot, as potentially visible to others.

## Allowed uses

Members may use AI tools to:

- explain an error message, a method, or a piece of code;
- draft or debug code, provided the member reads, tests, and understands every line before using it;
- suggest edits to grammar and clarity in text the member wrote;
- generate candidate research questions or search terms, which the member then checks against the literature and the data (this follows the *Designing Research Questions with GPT* exercise used in ECO225);
- summarize a paper the member will also read.

## Uses that are not allowed

Members may not:

- enter licensed, confidential, or administrative data, or any sample of it, into an AI tool;
- enter unpublished drafts, code, or results from a lab project into an external AI tool without the supervisor's permission;
- cite a source that an AI tool supplied without finding and reading the source;
- present AI-generated text, tables, or results as their own work in reports, papers, or presentations;
- use an AI tool to write a weekly report.

## Checking AI output

The member who uses an AI tool is responsible for its output. Before relying on it:

- run any code on a small test case where you know the answer;
- check that every reference exists and says what the tool claims;
- check that each number matches your own output files;
- make sure you could explain the step to your supervisor without the tool.

## Disclosure

Record AI use in your weekly report with one line: the tool, the task, and what you changed after checking. For example: "Used Copilot to draft the merge function; rewrote the key matching after it dropped duplicate IDs."

Papers and presentations from the lab state AI use in the acknowledgements or methods section, following the journal's or venue's rules. AI tools cannot be listed as authors, because they cannot take responsibility for the work ([Document 11](11-authorship-and-credit.md)).

## Why the rules exist

Weekly reports and supervisor meetings are where members learn to do research. If a tool does the reasoning, the member does not learn it, and the supervisor cannot see where help is needed. The lab's aim is for members to direct and check AI tools, a skill that matters more as the tools improve. The data rules exist because the lab's access to licensed and administrative data depends on keeping that data inside approved systems.

Ask the supervisor before using a tool if you are unsure whether a use is allowed.
