# 9. Writing Guide

Lab members write literature reviews, sections of working papers, and short policy briefs. This guide covers the structure of each and the habits that make economics writing clear.

## General rules

- Put the main point first, in the first sentence of a paragraph or section. Then give the support.
- Use short sentences and plain words. Define each technical term and abbreviation the first time you use it.
- Use the active voice where it is clearer: "We estimate" rather than "It is estimated."
- Give numbers with their units and a comparison: "Shelter use rose by 12 percent, from 8,000 to 8,960 nightly stays."
- Every claim needs support from data, a citation, or a stated assumption.
- Write a first draft quickly and revise it. Most of the quality comes from revision.

## Literature review

A literature review explains what is already known about a question and where the project fits.

1. **Organize by question or finding.** Group papers by what they find or the method they use. Do not summarize one paper per paragraph.
2. **Compare.** For each group, say where studies agree, where they disagree, and why (different data, period, place, or method).
3. **Locate the gap.** End with what the existing work does not answer and how the project addresses it.

Start from two or three recent, well-cited papers and follow their references backward and their citations forward (Google Scholar's "Cited by" link). Keep notes on each paper in the shared reference library: the question, data, method, main result, and one limitation.

## Sections of an empirical paper

| Section | What it does |
|---|---|
| Introduction | States the question, why it matters, what you do, what you find, and how it adds to existing work. Usually two to three pages. |
| Background | Describes the policy or setting the reader needs to follow the analysis. |
| Data | Sources, unit of observation, period, sample restrictions, and summary statistics. |
| Empirical strategy | The estimating equation, what each term means, and the assumption that gives the estimate a causal reading. |
| Results | Main estimates first, then robustness checks. Interpret the size of each effect, not only its sign and significance. |
| Conclusion | Summarizes findings, limits, and implications. Does not introduce new results. |

## Tables and figures

- Each table and figure should be readable without the text. Give it a full title, label the axes and units, and add a note on the data source and sample.
- Report standard errors and the number of observations.
- In the text, tell the reader what to look at: "Column 3 adds city fixed effects; the estimate falls by about half."

## Policy brief

A policy brief is two pages for a reader who is not an economist.

1. A one-paragraph summary of the finding and its implication.
2. The question and why it matters now.
3. What the data show, with one figure.
4. What this means for policy, stated with the appropriate level of confidence.
5. A short note on data and method, with a link to the full paper.

## Citations

Use author-year citations (Chicago or the style of the target journal). Manage references in Zotero or a BibTeX file in the repository. Cite datasets as well as papers.
